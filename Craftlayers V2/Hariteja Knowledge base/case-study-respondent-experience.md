## 1. Identity of the case

- **Title:** Three Questions Were Not the Problem
- **URL:** /work/respondent-experience
- **Category:** Respondent Experience · UX Research · Enterprise SaaS
- **Confidentiality:** Public · Anonymised
- **Hari's focus:** UX research and product design
- **Method:** Early qualitative study

**One-line summary:** A respondent-experience study showing why a short survey
still failed to earn sustained and honest participation.

**Central question:** What prevents people from repeatedly providing honest
feedback, even when a survey is short and easy to complete?

---

## 2. Approved facts (do not alter or combine)

- The evidence environment was an **internal recurring workplace-survey pilot**.
- Approximately **98 employees were invited**.
- In **one observed cycle**, approximately **8 employees responded**.
- Participation was **around 11% during the research period**.
- The survey had **three short questions** and took **under about two minutes**.
- Participation **declined across repeated iterations**.

**Number handling rules.**
Keep these figures approximate and separate. Never compute or state
"8 out of 98 equals 11%". Never present the figures as a universal or
statistically representative result. Always say "approximately" / "around".

## 4. The core reframe

The dashboard built on this survey was producing limited value because the
response base was too small for confident interpretation. Because the survey
itself was only three questions, interface complexity did not explain the low
participation. The problem was therefore reframed from a *dashboard-value*
problem into an upstream **respondent-experience and trust** problem.

Diagnostic chain: low participation → initial assumption of survey friction →
evidence of a three-question, two-minute survey → underlying issue is the
**respondent contract**.

---

## 5. The five respondent conditions (the "respondent contract")

1. **Relevance** — Why does this matter, and why am I being asked again?
2. **Effort** — What does responding require beyond the time spent filling the form?
3. **Safety** — Can I answer honestly without being personally exposed?
4. **Impact** — What happens after I submit?
5. **Ownership** — Who is responsible for acting?

The pilot was weak on most of these. This framework is the signature idea of
the case study — lead with it when someone asks "what did he find?".

---

## 6. The respondent journey (and where it broke)

Invitation ("Another workplace email.") → Reminder ("What is this about
again?") → Survey ("Is my answer really anonymous?") → Submit ("Who will see
this?") → Silence ("Did anything happen?") → Next cycle ("Why should I answer
again?").

**Two primary experience breaks:** between *invitation and reminder*, and
between *submission and any visible impact*.

---

## 7. Main findings

- The survey was simple, but the programme was unclear.
- The reminder email became the real entry point.
- Welcome and reminder communication were disconnected.
- Weekly repetition was not sufficiently explained.
- "Anonymous" was stated but not explained in practice.
- Small-team identifiability created hesitation.
- Open-text responses felt personally traceable.
- Respondents did not know what happened after submitting.
- There was no visible impact or closure loop.
- Repeated negatively framed questions could contribute to negative recall.
- Positive answers could feel safer than honest answers.
- Participation alone was not enough; trust and honesty also mattered.
- Governance and manager action ownership were blurred.

**Language rule.** Use "cognitive and emotional burden", "emotional discomfort"
or "negative recall". Never use clinical or psychological diagnosis language.

---

## 8. Evidence → insight → decision (the four key decisions)

1. **Evidence:** The reminder became the actual entry point.
   **Insight:** The experience could not depend on the welcome being remembered.
   **Decision:** Make every reminder independently understandable.

2. **Evidence:** "Anonymous" was stated but not explained in practice.
   **Insight:** An abstract privacy claim did not create felt safety.
   **Decision:** Explain grouped reporting, open-text handling and
   minimum-response protection.

3. **Evidence:** Respondents saw no visible impact after submitting.
   **Insight:** A recurring request had not earned the next response.
   **Decision:** Add a visible closure loop.

4. **Evidence:** Governance and manager action ownership were blurred.
   **Insight:** No role clearly owned the "what happens next?".
   **Decision:** Separate governance from action ownership.

---

## 9. The broken feedback loop

Ask → Respond → Protect and aggregate → Interpret → Act → **Communicate
impact** → Ask again.

"Communicate impact" was the missing step. Consequence chain: no visible action
→ lower trust → weaker future participation.

Closing principle: **a recurring survey must earn the next response.**

---

## 10. Ownership model

- **Respondent** — provides honest feedback.
- **Manager / Action Owner** — interprets team-level insight, discusses themes,
  takes action and communicates closure.
- **HR / Governance** — governs the programme, protects anonymity, monitors
  organisation-level patterns and enables managers.
- **Leadership** — reviews systemic patterns and supports organisation-level action.
- **System** — aggregates responses, protects thresholds and supports communication.

**Principle:** when governance owns the entire activity, it risks becoming an HR
survey; when managers own follow-up, it becomes a management practice.

**Never state that HR owns employee performance.**

---

## 11. Recommended future-state journey

Explain purpose → Invite safely → Make completion easy → Protect and aggregate
→ Interpret at the right level → Act → Communicate what changed → Earn the next
response.

---

## 12. Anonymity — how to answer privacy questions

Respondent questions that came up: Can my manager infer it was me? What happens
in a small team? Is my written response shown directly? Who sees raw answers?
When are results hidden?

**Approved answer direction:** responses are combined into group-level patterns;
individual feedback is not shown as a named response; reporting appears only
when privacy conditions are met, and a **minimum group threshold applies**.

**Never** publish a specific numeric threshold. **Never** claim guaranteed
anonymity.

---

## 13. Status of every recommendation — critical

Every item below is a **recommendation only**. None has shipped. None has
post-change measurement. **None is validated.** Never imply that participation
improved or that the problem was solved.

- Self-contained reminder communication
- Clearer cadence explanation
- Practical anonymity explanation
- Visible impact communication
- Neutral or "No blocker this week" path
- Question-framing review
- Grouped reporting explanation
- Manager / HR ownership separation

---

## 14. Limitations (state these if asked about rigour)

- The study was early and directional, with a small interview sample and no
  broad quantitative validation.
- Conducted in one internal organisation.
- Management input was stakeholder feedback, not a formal interview.
- Findings are directional hypotheses, not statistically representative.
- No post-recommendation measurement exists.

---

## 15. On survey format (a common follow-up question)

Shorter surveys reduce interaction effort. Conversational or AI-driven surveys
may improve depth or engagement. **Neither solves** unclear purpose, weak
privacy communication, missing impact or absent ownership on its own. Any
format still depends on a credible respondent contract.

---

## 16. Reflection / what this demonstrates about Hari

Participation was the visible symptom; underneath sat a system of purpose,
trust, honesty, visible impact and ownership. The main contribution was
diagnostic — reframing a dashboard-value problem as an upstream
respondent-experience problem, and being disciplined about separating what the
evidence confirmed from what it only suggested.

Skills this case demonstrates: qualitative research, evidence synthesis,
product judgement, systems thinking, privacy-aware design, stakeholder
alignment, and honest reporting of unvalidated work.
